Ohm’s Law, Conductance & Power Dissipation Characteristics

★★★★★ 01. Core Theory & Concept · Class Note · Lecture 04 (Professor Board Derivation & Curve Analysis)

  • \(v = iR\) (under PSC), Conductance \(G = \frac{1}{R} = \frac{i}{v}\) (Siemens), and non-negative resistor power \(p = i^{2}R = \frac{v^{2}}{R} \ge 0\).
  • Ohm’s Law states that the voltage \(v\) across a linear resistor is directly proportional to the current \(i\) flowing through it, provided the current enters the positive terminal \((v = +iR)\).
  • If the reference current enters the negative terminal, \(v = -iR\).
  • Conductance \(G\) is the reciprocal of resistance \((G = \frac{1}{R})\), measured in Siemens (S) or mhos (℧).
  • Because a passive resistor cannot generate energy, its absorbed power \(p = i^{2}R = v^{2}G\) is always non-negative regardless of voltage or current polarity.
Ohm’s Law (Voltage & Current Forms) (\(V, A, \Omega , S\))
\(v = i \cdot R ⟺ i = G \cdot v = \frac{v}{R}\)
Resistor Power Dissipation (Watts \((W \ge 0)\))
\(p = v \cdot i = i^{2}R = \frac{v^{2}}{R} = v^{2}G = \frac{i^{2}}{G}\)

Class tip: Remember the two extreme limits of resistance: a Short Circuit has \(R = 0 \Omega (G \to \infty S)\), meaning \(v = 0 V\) for any finite current \(i\); an Open Circuit has \(R \to \infty \Omega (G = 0 S)\), meaning \(i = 0\) A for any finite voltage \(v\).

Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL) & Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL)

★★★★★ 02. Core Theory & Concept · Class Note · Lecture 05 (Kirchhoff’s Laws & Two-Loop T-Network)

  • KCL (Conservation of Charge): \(\sum i_{in} = \sum i_{out}\) at any node.
  • KVL (Conservation of Energy): \(\sum v_{m} = 0\) around any closed path.
  • Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL) is based on the conservation of electric charge: a node cannot store net charge, so the algebraic sum of currents entering a node (or closed Gaussian boundary) must equal zero at every instant.
  • Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL) is based on the conservation of energy: the work done moving a unit charge around a complete closed loop returns to the same potential, so the algebraic sum of all branch voltages around any closed loop is identically zero.
Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL) (Amperes (A))
\(\sum _{n=1}^{N} i_{n} = 0 ⟺ \sum i_{entering} = \sum i_{leaving}\)
Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL) (Volts (V))
\(\sum _{m=1}^{M} v_{m} = 0 ⟺ \sum v_{drops} = \sum v_{rises}\)
Fundamental Theorem of Network Topology (\(b =\) branches, \(l =\) independent loops, \(n =\) nodes)
\(b = l + n - 1\)

Bridge Equivalent Resistance Using \(\Delta \to Y\) Transformation

★★★★ 04. Previous Year Question (PYQ) · Previous Year Question · Final Exam 2022 Q2(c) & 2020 Q2(b) · Asked in: 2022, 2020 (8 Marks)

  • Find total equivalent resistance \(R_{a}b\) and source current \(I_{s}\) for an unbalanced 5-resistor bridge.
Problem / Theorem Question
  • An unbalanced bridge circuit connected to a 24 V DC source consists of upper delta resistors \(R_{a} = 30 \Omega , R_{b} = 10 \Omega , R_{c} = 20 \Omega \), and lower arm resistors \(R_{l}eft = 11 \Omega \) and \(R_{r}ight = 7 \Omega \). Find the total equivalent resistance \(R_{a}b\) and the source current \(I_{s}\).

Given parameters

  • Upper \(\Delta \) network: \(R_{a} = 30 \Omega , R_{b} = 10 \Omega , R_{c} = 20 \Omega \) (Sum \(= 60 \Omega \))
  • Lower series arms after \(\Delta \to Y\) conversion: \(R_{l}eft = 11 \Omega , R_{r}ight = 7 \Omega \)
  • Applied DC source voltage \(V_{s} = 24 V\)
Step 1: Convert the upper \(\Delta (R_{a}, R_{b}, R_{c})\) into an equivalent \(Y (R_{1}, R_{2}, R_{3})\)

Using the \(\Delta \to Y\) denominator \(R_{a} + R_{b} + R_{c} = 30 + 10 + 20 = 60 \Omega \):

\(R_{1}\) (top) \(= \frac{R_{b}\cdot R_{c}}{60}\)

\(= \frac{10\times 20}{60}\)

\(= 3.33 \Omega \)

\(R_{2}\) (left) \(= \frac{R_{a}\cdot R_{c}}{60}\)

\(= \frac{30\times 20}{60}\)

\(= 10 \Omega \)

\(R_{3}\) (right) \(= \frac{R_{a}\cdot R_{b}}{60}\)

\(= \frac{30\times 10}{60}\)

\(= 5 \Omega \)

Step 2: Combine series & parallel branches with lower arms

Left branch \(R_{L} = R_{2} + 11 = 10 + 11 = 21 \Omega \); Right branch \(R_{R} = R_{3} + 7 = 5 + 7 = 12 \Omega \):

\(R_{p}arallel = \frac{21 \times 12}{21 + 12}\)

\(= \frac{252}{33}\)

\(= 7.64 \Omega \)

\(R_{a}b = R_{1} + R_{p}arallel\)

\(= 3.33 + 7.64\)

\(= 10.97 \Omega \)

Step 3: Calculate total source current \(I_{s}\)

Apply Ohm’s Law at the input terminals:

\(I_{s} = \frac{V_{s}}{R_{a}b}\)

\(= 24 \frac{V}{10.97} \Omega \)

\(= 2.19 A\)

∴ \(R_{a}b = 10.97 \Omega , I_{s} = 2.19 A\)

Ladder Network Reduction & Current Divider Branch Analysis

★★★ 05. Worked Numerical Example · Professor-Followed Book · Alexander & Sadiku (6th Ed.), Ch. 2

  • Reduce a resistive ladder from the load end back to the source, then use current division at each node.
Problem / Theorem Question
  • In a resistive ladder connected to a 30 V source, series resistor \(R_{1} = 4 \Omega \) feeds a parallel combination of \(R_{2} = 6 \Omega \) and a right-hand branch of \(R_{3} = 2 \Omega \) in series with \(R_{4} = 4 \Omega \). Find the total equivalent resistance \(R_{eq}\), source current \(I_{s}\), and current \(I_{4}\) through \(R_{4}\).
Step 1: Collapse ladder from right to left

Combine \(R_{3} + R_{4}\) in series, then in parallel with \(R_{2}\):

\(R_{34} = R_{3} + R_{4}\)

\(= 2 + 4\)

\(= 6 \Omega \)

\(R_{p} = \frac{R_{2} \times R_{34}}{R_{2} + R_{34}}\)

\(= \frac{6 \times 6}{12}\)

\(= 3 \Omega \)

∴ \(R_{eq} = R_{1} + R_{p} = 4 + 3 = 7 \Omega \)

Step 2: Find source current \(I_{s}\) and branch current \(I_{4}\)

Apply Ohm’s Law and the Current Divider Rule:

\(I_{s} = \frac{30}{7}\)

\(= 4.29 A\)

\(I_{4} = I_{s} \cdot [ \frac{R_{2}}{R_{2} + R_{34}} ]\)

∴ \(I_{4} = 4.29 \times (\frac{6}{12}) = 2.14 A\)

Balanced Wheatstone Bridge Condition & Zero-Galvanometer Current Proof

★ 06. Core Theory & Concept · Syllabus Book · Robert L. Boylestad (Introductory Circuit Analysis, Ch. 7)

  • A 4-arm Wheatstone bridge is balanced \((I_{G} = 0 A)\) when the products of opposite arm resistances are equal: \(R_{1}R_{4} = R_{2}R_{3}\).
Balanced Wheatstone Bridge Ratio
\(\frac{R_{1}}{R_{2}} = \frac{R_{3}}{R_{4}} ⟺ R_{1} R_{4} = R_{2} R_{3}\) (when \(I_{G} = 0\))
